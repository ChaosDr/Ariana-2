AN IMMERSIVE CASE STUDY EXERCISE

Ripples: Ariana, Part II

You are the therapist again. Several weeks into treatment, Ariana is still composed and clipped, but something has come into focus: a faint spark whenever other people's conflicts come up. A friend was hurt by something she said, a relationship has ended, and her social circle has quietly taken sides. This episode stands on its own, so you do not need to have played Part I.

Three gauges track her: Detachment, how far she retreats into flatness or her phone, Trust, how safe she feels with you, and Awareness, how clearly she registers her effect on other people. Awareness only rises when she is invited to look at her impact and feels safe enough to try. Push too hard and she goes elsewhere. Join her story and nothing moves. There is no perfect script.

Your session, moment by moment

The numbers appear here for the first time. Notice that the game never tells you whether the rumor was deliberate. Both readings stay open on purpose: your job in the group discussion is to say what you would need to know to tell them apart.

Reading your trace

  • Where did awareness actually move, and what had to be in place first (trust, timing, how the question was framed)?
  • Which choices felt like care but left the ripples invisible? Which felt like honesty but made her leave the room?
  • Did you reach for a label? What did it explain, and what did it stop you from asking?

Take it to your group

Before anything else: choose one detail from Ariana's story that you find important or puzzling, and invite each person in your group to share theirs. (If nothing jumps out, try the "dad" slips: the case is explicit that it is not romantic. What might it mean, and what else could explain it?)

Your task: build an interpretation of what Ariana is struggling with and what might reach her. Several interpretations may fit, so use details from the case to explain yours. Start with a question that interests your group. You don't need to finish every question or agree with each other.

  • She told her friend something false about the boyfriend, gave vague justifications, and seemed genuinely confused that the couple would address it together. Before you judge, construct the most charitable interpretation of what happened, then the least charitable. What is the difference between someone who manipulates deliberately and someone who does not register how social ripples work?
  • After the breakup, her framing of her ex gradually shifted the group, and he was not invited to gatherings, though she did not seem to orchestrate it consciously. How does influence work when it doesn't look like influence? What does this tell us about how narratives spread and whose version of events tends to win?
  • She financially supported a partner she describes as unsupportive, emotionally distant, and struggling with addiction, and received, by her account, no gratitude. What might she have been getting, or hoping to get? What might make sense from the inside, even if it looked puzzling from outside?
  • "Emotionally stunted" or something harder to name? She has been on medication for a decade and in treatment, and people still describe her the same way. Before reaching for a label, if one is even needed, what do you think is actually going on inside her? How do you hold compassion for someone whose behavior causes real harm to the people closest to them?
  • What does she actually need? Setting aside the diagnoses, the dark triad speculation, and the frustration of the people around her, if you try to see her as someone genuinely finding something about life hard to navigate, what do you think that thing is? What would it take for someone in her life to actually reach her?

When you have an initial answer, stay with it a little longer:

  • What in the story makes us think that?
  • What else could explain the same behavior?
  • What would we need to know to tell the difference?

For example, "she's manipulative" is a starting point. Does she intend the effect, fail to register it, or not care about it? Would those possibilities call for the same response? If your group reaches for a personality disorder label, test it the same way: what supports it, what else could produce the same behavior, and what would we need to know to tell the difference?

Changing your mind, questioning an interpretation, and noticing missing information all count as contributions. You don't need to share personal experiences. Please discuss without AI. No written answers are required.
